# Reproeconomics: the economic impact of state-funded oocyte cryopreservation

This repository contains the code and replication materials for the paper:

**Alejandro Corchón Franco**  
*Reproeconomics: the economic impact of state-funded oocyte cryopreservation*

Final Year Project written at **Universitat Pompeu Fabra**, under the supervision of **Guillem López i Casasnovas, PhD**.

## Introduction

Spain has experienced a sustained decline in fertility together with a marked postponement of childbearing. In this context, elective oocyte cryopreservation (OC) has increasingly been discussed as a possible policy instrument to mitigate the biological constraints associated with delayed motherhood. This project evaluates whether publicly funded elective OC could increase fertility and satisfy a long-run fiscal sustainability condition in Spain.

The paper has three main objectives. First, it examines whether publicly funded access to elective OC is likely to improve fertility outcomes in the medium term. Second, it evaluates whether such a policy could be self-financing in the long run, in the sense that any additional births generated by the intervention would produce a fiscal return comparable to programme cost. Third, it develops an economic evaluation of the proposed programme through an incremental cost-effectiveness analysis, a budget impact assessment, and a distributive analysis of alternative funding designs.

## Methodology

The empirical analysis combines annual demographic and macroeconomic data for Spain over the period **1976–2022**. The demographic component uses an Interrupted Time Series (ITS) framework to identify structural changes in fertility dynamics around an endogenously defined breakpoint. The paper treats the 2010 breakpoint as a hypothetical anchor for the counterfactual, rather than as the date of an actual policy reform, since no publicly funded elective OC programme was implemented in Spain during the estimation window.

The annual dataset integrates Spanish demographic and macroeconomic series from official sources, including fertility outcomes, age-specific fertility rates, parity-related indicators, exposure variables, and macroeconomic controls. The sample covers **47 annual observations** for Spain.

The second analytical component is an economic evaluation based on a decision-analytic cost model. This framework incorporates uptake, utilisation behaviour, age-stratified success rates, and a demographic additionality parameter in order to estimate expected cost per additional live birth, fiscal sustainability, annual budget impact, and the distributional consequences of alternative public funding designs.

In its baseline form, the demographic specification is:

Y_t = β_0 + β_1 t + β_2 D_t + β_3 (t − T_0)D_t + u_t

where \(Y_t\) denotes the fertility outcome of interest, \(D_t\) is the post-break indicator, and \(T_0\) is the structural breakpoint.

The economic evaluation is centred on the expected cost per additional live birth:

Cost per birth = (C_u + uC_T) / (usα)

where \(C_u\) is the vitrification cost, \(C_T\) is the thaw-and-transfer cost, \(u\) is the utilisation rate, \(s\) is the success rate, and \(\alpha\) is the demographic additionality parameter.

## Discussion

The demographic analysis identifies a large and statistically significant negative level shift in adjusted total fertility at the 2010 breakpoint, with no subsequent convergence towards the pre-break counterfactual. Age-specific evidence suggests an asymmetry in post-break fertility dynamics: fertility at ages 35–39 shows partial recovery consistent with postponement, whereas fertility at ages 40–44 remains below its counterfactual trajectory. This pattern is not consistent with complete intertemporal substitution and suggests that delayed childbearing does not restore aggregate fertility.

The economic evaluation indicates that publicly funded elective OC is unlikely to be fiscally self-sustaining under empirically plausible parameter values. In the base case, the estimated cost per additional live birth is far above the inflation-adjusted net fiscal contribution benchmark used in the paper, and the implied fiscal return per euro spent remains below break-even. The paper therefore concludes that elective OC may be relevant as a policy of redistribution and access, but not as a credible pro-natalist mechanism at the aggregate level.
