\begin{center}
{\large\bfseries Reproeconomics: The Economic Impact of State-Funded
Oocyte Cryopreservation}
\end{center}

\vspace{0.8em}

% TODO: Add acknowledgements if required

\noindent\textbf{Abstract}

\noindent Spain's persistent fertility decline has prompted political
proposals to publicly fund elective oocyte cryopreservation (OC) as a
pro-natalist instrument. Drawing on a decision-analytic cost model
calibrated with empirically observed utilisation rates, age-stratified
success probabilities, and a generational accounting benchmark, this
paper evaluates whether such a programme satisfies a fiscal
self-sustainability condition. The marginal cost per additional live
birth ranges from \euro{}137,500 (optimistic) to above
\euro{}1,000,000 (pessimistic), exceeding the net fiscal contribution
of an OC-conceived individual under all plausible parameter
combinations. The programme is not self-financing; the valid
justification for public funding is redistribution of reproductive
access alone, not fiscal sustainability.

\vspace{0.5em}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} Elective oocyte cryopreservation;
Cost-effectiveness; Health technology assessment; Fiscal sustainability;
Spain

\vspace{0.5em}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------

Spain's demographic trajectory is among the most acute in the
developed world. According to Spain's National Statistics Institute,
318,005 births occurred in 2024, the lowest number since the
statistics series started in 1941 and a decline of almost 25\% over
the previous decade \citep{ine2025mnp,funcas2024}. Women gave birth to
their first child at an average age of 32.6 years, compared to 25.6
years in 1980 \citep{funcas2024}. Against this backdrop, the number of
women undergoing elective oocyte cryopreservation (OC) in Spain
increased almost eighteenfold between 2011 and 2021
\citep{sef2024registro}, and Spain records the highest share of births
to mothers aged 40 and over in the European Union, at 10.7\% of all
live births in 2021 \citep{blas2025}.

The Popular Party's policy statement, approved at its July congress,
includes for the first time public subsidies for egg freezing for women
who wish to delay motherhood \citep{blas2025,partidopopular2025}. The
proposal is modelled after the Galician government's programme
providing publicly funded OC for women aged 30--35 under reproductive
health criteria \citep{xunta2024}. Public funding for elective OC
remains the exception in European health systems, where reimbursement
is typically limited to medical indications such as fertility
preservation prior to gonadotoxic therapy
\citep{shenfield2017,gambadauro2023}. Existing studies assess OC
either from a clinical perspective or under individual-level economic
models; evidence on its cost-effectiveness and fiscal sustainability
when evaluated at the population level remains scarce.

If clinical IVF is already publicly funded for medical indications,
the case for extending that logic to elective OC rests either on
equity grounds---reducing the large socioeconomic gradient in private
access documented by \citet{alonpinilla2021}---or on fiscal grounds,
namely that additional births financed by the programme will generate a
positive net return to the public purse. Assessing the fiscal
dimension rigorously is the purpose of this paper.

This paper provides an economic evaluation of publicly funded elective
OC as proposed within the Spanish national policy debate. Specifically,
it constructs a decision-analytic cost model to estimate: (i) the cost
per additional live birth and an incremental cost-effectiveness ratio
(ICER) relative to the status quo of private financing; (ii) the
annual budget impact under alternative uptake scenarios; and (iii) the
distributional consequences of universal versus means-tested funding
designs. The paper asks whether the programme satisfies a fiscal
self-sustainability condition---whether additional births generated by
the intervention yield a return to the public purse at least comparable
to programme costs.

The remainder of the paper is structured as follows.
Section~\ref{sec:literature} reviews the relevant literature.
Section~\ref{sec:model} describes the cost-effectiveness model.
Section~\ref{sec:results} presents the results.
Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses the findings and their policy
implications. Tables and figures appear at the end of the manuscript;
Appendix~\ref{app:decisiontree} contains the decision-tree
representation of the model.

\section{Literature Review}\label{sec:literature}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------

The literature converges on two findings that are central to the policy
question examined here. First, access to assisted reproductive
technologies (ART) is extremely uneven and strongly correlated with
socioeconomic position. \citet{alonpinilla2021} demonstrate, using
microdata from the Spanish \emph{Encuesta de Fecundidad}, that access
to ART and the choice between public and private provision are
significantly influenced by income and education: women from lower
socioeconomic backgrounds are more likely to be denied access even when
they report reproductive issues, while women treated solely in the
private sector display a greater cumulative pregnancy incidence than
those treated in the public system (2021:15). Similar dynamics are
documented at the European level by \citet{seiz2023}, who find
significant cross-national disparities consistently and negatively
affecting women from lower socioeconomic backgrounds.

Second, the demand for elective OC is dominated by a preference for
option value rather than for a definite birth. The most frequently
stated reasons for non-medical OC, according to a systematic review by
\citet{kynigopoulou2024}, are the lack of a suitable partner,
investments in education and career, and general economic
uncertainties. \citet{gambadauro2023}, drawing on survey data from
Swedish university students, find that OC is broadly accepted as a
technology but that support for public funding is significantly lower
when OC is presented as age-related rather than medically required; OC
is viewed predominantly as reproductive insurance rather than as a
fertility enhancer.

From an economic standpoint, the effectiveness of elective OC depends
critically on the subsequent utilisation of stored oocytes. The
recorded utilisation rates in the published cohort literature are
consistently low. \citet{yang2022}, in a retrospective cohort of 2,038
women followed over seven years, found that only 8.4\% returned to use
their vitrified oocytes, yielding a population-level birth rate of just
3.0\% relative to all women who froze. In a Spanish private clinic
sample of 1,241 women, \citet{cobo2018} reported a 12.1\% return rate
over a median follow-up of 4.2 years; rates are as low as 5\% in
United Kingdom private settings \citep{trokoudes2011}. The cost per
live delivery rises substantially when expenditures are averaged among
all freezing women rather than only those who eventually use OC
\citep{yang2022}: because utilisation is a revealed preference that a
price subsidy will only partially shift, programme cost must be spread
over all recipients, not only those who ultimately attempt a pregnancy.

The formal cost-effectiveness literature reaches qualified conclusions.
\citet{loendersloot2011}, in a Markov model contrasting elective OC at
age 35 with no freezing followed by subsequent IVF, find that OC is
cost-effective only under stringent assumptions, requiring high
utilisation rates and favourable success probabilities simultaneously.
\citet{bakkensen2022}, drawing on US SART-CORS data, find that OC at a
younger age can lower the expected cost per live delivery among women
who delay childbearing, but sensitivity analyses reveal these findings
depend heavily on assumptions about freezing age and future use.
Positive outcomes at the individual level do not always translate to a
population-wide, publicly financed solution.

A particularly relevant body of literature takes a public finance
perspective. \citet{matorras2016} estimate the lifetime net fiscal
contribution (NFC) of individuals born through assisted reproduction in
Spain using a generational accounting methodology. In the base case,
the NFC of an IVF-conceived individual is \euro{}66,709 (at 2006
prices), implying a return of \euro{}15.98 per euro of public
investment: the discounted value of future taxes and social
contributions surpasses public spending despite significant upfront
medical expenditures (2016:118). This figure serves as the principal
benchmark against which the programme cost model in
Section~\ref{sec:model} is calibrated. International evidence
similarly shows positive lifetime fiscal contributions for ART-conceived
individuals in most countries, with the notable exception of the
Netherlands \citep{moolenaar2014}.

The analytic step not undertaken by \citet{matorras2016} is to embed
the NFC estimate within a complete cost model that incorporates the
vitrification cost layer specific to elective OC, the empirically
observed utilisation rate, the age-stratified success rate at thaw, and
a demographic additionality factor---the share of OC-assisted births
that would not have occurred by any other means. Once these four
adjustments are applied, the effective fiscal return per euro of public
OC expenditure falls considerably below the \euro{}15.98 reported for
clinical IVF. That exercise is the contribution of the present paper.

\section{The Cost-Effectiveness Model}\label{sec:model}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------

\subsection{Framework and cost parameters}\label{sec:framework}

OC is best conceptualised as a sequence of clinically established yet
economically heterogeneous stages. The process begins with a
gynaecologic diagnostic and eligibility assessment, followed by
controlled ovarian stimulation using exogenous gonadotropins over
approximately 10--14 days---the most resource-intensive component.
Once adequate follicular maturation is achieved, oocyte retrieval is
performed via ultrasound-guided aspiration under light anaesthesia, and
retrieved oocytes are vitrified for long-term storage in liquid
nitrogen \citep{pai2021}. Should the woman subsequently decide to
attempt a pregnancy, she undergoes a separate thaw-and-transfer IVF
cycle.

The official cost for assisted reproduction in the Spanish public
health system is not documented. Cost inputs are drawn from published
estimates of direct healthcare costs for IVF cycles in two Spanish
institutions \citep{matorrasweinig2011,matorras2001,navarro2006}. Based
on an average success rate of 28.7\% \citep{sef2001}, the expected
investment per live IVF birth is \euro{}4,173 (2006 prices), reflecting
that 75\% of pregnancies end in a live birth; delivery costs are
excluded as they are presumed constant between assisted and naturally
conceived children. Three caveats qualify the application of these
figures to elective OC. First, all \citet{matorras2016} values are in
2006 prices; Spanish healthcare input costs rose by about 37\% between
2006 and 2023 (INE IPCSNA), so the inflation-adjusted NFC is
approximately \euro{}91,391 at 2023 prices. Second, the 28.7\% success
rate reflects clinical IVF for infertility and does not transfer
directly to elective OC, since age at retrieval, age at thaw, and
number of vitrified oocytes interact non-linearly
\citep{cobo2016,chronopoulou2021}. Third, elective OC adds a cost
element absent from clinical IVF: the vitrification cycle itself
(stimulation, retrieval, laboratory processing, multi-year
cryostorage), approximated at \euro{}3,000--5,000 at SEF 2023 prices.
This cost is incurred regardless of whether the woman ever uses her
oocytes, making it a fixed programme cost that must be included in any
complete budget-impact analysis.

\subsection{Utilisation rates, success rates, and the additionality
parameter}\label{sec:parameters}

The percentage of women who actually use their vitrified oocytes is
the sole major driver of overall programme cost-effectiveness. As
documented in Section~\ref{sec:literature}, empirical utilisation rates
are consistently well below levels typically assumed by programme
designers: 8.4\% \citep{yang2022}, 12.1\% in Spain
\citep{cobo2018}, and as low as 5\% in United Kingdom private settings
\citep{trokoudes2011}. Low utilisation is not a market failure
correctable via subsidy; it reflects the preferences of users who
value the option, not the outcome \citep{kynigopoulou2024}.

Success rates also differ materially from the clinical IVF average.
\citet{cobo2016} estimate that a woman who vitrifies five oocytes has a
cumulative live birth probability of approximately 22\% (at age
$< 35$), compared to 72\% if fifteen or more mature oocytes are
obtained. \citet{chronopoulou2021} document that live birth rates per
vitrified oocyte decline sharply with age. The frequently reported
average success rate of 28.7\% therefore substantially overestimates
outcomes for women who freeze fewer than ten oocytes or who do so after
age 37, which is the modal profile of elective users. A success rate of
$s = 0.30$ is adopted for the base case, with sensitivity analysis
extended to the range $[0.20,\,0.40]$.

The demographic additionality parameter $\alpha$---the share of
OC-assisted births that would not have occurred by any other means---is
the most uncertain parameter in the model and cannot be directly
observed. It is bounded by three considerations drawn from the
available evidence. First, women who use their stored oocytes may have
conceived naturally or via other ART in the absence of the programme,
in which case the OC-assisted birth does not represent a net increase
in completed fertility. Second, the consistently low return-to-use
rates documented across multiple cohort studies imply that the scope
for additional births is limited to a small fraction of programme
participants; at the population level, OC generates one live birth per
approximately 33 women who freeze at the best-documented utilisation
and success rates. Third, qualitative and survey evidence consistently
indicates that elective OC is used predominantly as reproductive
insurance---an option that reduces anxiety rather than enabling births
that would not otherwise occur \citep{kynigopoulou2024,gambadauro2023}.
These considerations support $\alpha \in [0.30,\,0.50]$ as the base
case, with sensitivity extended to $[0.20,\,0.70]$.

\subsection{Cost per additional live birth and fiscal
sustainability}\label{sec:cost}

Let $C_u$ denote the cost of a vitrification cycle including five-year
storage (\euro{}5,000 at SEF 2023 prices), $C_T$ the cost of a
thaw-and-transfer IVF cycle (\euro{}2,500), $u$ the utilisation rate,
$s$ the success rate per thaw cycle, and $\alpha$ the demographic
additionality factor. The expected cost per additional live birth is

\begin{equation}
\text{Cost per birth} = \frac{C_u + u\,C_T}{u\,s\,\alpha}.
\label{eq:cost}
\end{equation}

\noindent The numerator is the total expected public expenditure per
woman who freezes: the fixed vitrification cost plus the expected
thaw-and-transfer cost incurred by the fraction $u$ who return to use
their oocytes. The denominator is the expected number of additional
live births per woman who freezes, equal to the product of utilisation
rate, clinical success rate, and additionality factor. A public
programme is fiscally self-sustaining if and only if the expected cost
per additional birth does not exceed the net fiscal contribution (NFC)
of the additional individual over the life cycle. The fiscal
self-sustainability condition is

\begin{equation}
u\,s\,\alpha \;\geq\; \frac{C_u + u\,C_T}{\mathrm{NFC}}.
\label{eq:fiscal}
\end{equation}

\noindent Because $u$ appears on both sides, this condition is
nonlinear in $u$ and must be evaluated numerically. At the
inflation-adjusted NFC of \euro{}91,391 (2023 prices), with $C_u =
\euro{}5{,}000$ and $C_T = \euro{}2{,}500$, the required
break-even product $u\,s\,\alpha$ can be calculated for any pair of
clinical and behavioural assumptions.

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------

\subsection{Cost-effectiveness and sensitivity analysis}\label{sec:costeff}

At the base-case parameters ($u = 0.08$, $s = 0.30$, $\alpha = 0.50$),
the expected cost per additional live birth is \euro{}433,333. The
gross fiscal return per euro of public expenditure is therefore
approximately \euro{}0.21 (\euro{}91,391 / \euro{}433,333)---well below
break-even and dramatically below the \euro{}15.98 return estimated for
clinical IVF by \citet{matorras2016}. Under the pessimistic scenario
($u = 0.05$, $s = 0.20$), costs per birth exceed \euro{}1,025,000 and
the fiscal return falls to approximately \euro{}0.09 per euro,
implying a structural fiscal loss of more than \euro{}0.91 per euro of
public expenditure.

Figure~\ref{fig:cost_sensitivity} presents a bidimensional sensitivity
analysis over the plausible ranges of $u$ and $s$ with $\alpha$ held
at 0.50. Costs range from \euro{}137,500 (optimistic: $u = 0.20$,
$s = 0.40$) to above \euro{}1,000,000 (pessimistic: $u = 0.05$,
$s = 0.20$). At no point in the plausible parameter space does the
cost per birth fall below the \euro{}91,391 inflation-adjusted NFC.
In the empirical utilisation range (5--12\%), the cost per additional
birth lies between \euro{}433,333 and \euro{}683,333, far exceeding any
defensible willingness-to-pay threshold for a publicly funded
pro-natalist intervention.

\FloatBarrier

The deterministic sensitivity analysis in Figure~\ref{fig:tornado}
confirms that the utilisation rate $u$ is by far the primary
uncertainty driver: varying $u$ from 5\% to 20\% alone shifts the
cost per birth by more than \euro{}500,000, dwarfing the sensitivity
to any other parameter. The additionality factor $\alpha$ and the
success rate $s$ are the next most influential parameters; cost inputs
($C_u$, $C_T$) have comparatively modest effects.

\FloatBarrier

Turning to the fiscal self-sustainability condition
(Equation~\eqref{eq:fiscal}), at the inflation-adjusted NFC of
\euro{}91,391 and holding $\alpha = 0.60$ and $s = 0.35$ at their
upper plausible bounds, the break-even utilisation rate is
$u \approx 0.30$---approximately 3.8 times the empirical average.
Under the conservative 2006 NFC of \euro{}66,709 (without inflation
adjustment), the required utilisation rises to $u \approx 0.43$, more
than five times the observed average. The programme thus fails the
fiscal self-sustainability condition on either price basis by a large
margin at any empirically plausible parameter combination.

\subsection{Budget impact}\label{sec:budget}

Spain has approximately 1.8 million women aged 30--35
\citep{ine2023}. Figure~\ref{fig:budget_scenarios} illustrates three
uptake scenarios and the corresponding annual budgetary commitment. At
5\% uptake, nearly 90,000 women would freeze annually; at the stated
vitrification cost of \euro{}5,000 per cycle, gross vitrification
expenditure alone amounts to \euro{}450M per year. Adding expected
thaw-and-transfer costs (approximately \euro{}18M at 8\% utilisation
and \euro{}2,500 per cycle) and administration (\euro{}2M), annual
programme costs total approximately \euro{}470M. Under a 10\% uptake
scenario, annual costs reach approximately \euro{}938M; at 20\%
uptake, they exceed \euro{}1.87Bn, equivalent to nearly 2.3\% of total
Spanish public health expenditure \citep{minsan2024}. Adverse event
costs (Section~\ref{sec:adverse}) are excluded from these estimates
and would add a further 5--8\% to direct procedure costs.

These figures warrant explicit comparison with alternative uses of
public funds. Spain currently spends around \euro{}1.9Bn annually on
family and child policy \citep{oecd2024}. Comparative demographic
evidence indicates that affordable childcare, wage-replacement parental
leave, and housing support interventions achieve stronger and more
consistent effects on completed fertility than ART-based programmes
\citep{gauthier2007,rindfuss2010}. Even the minimum (5\% uptake)
scenario would consume approximately \euro{}470M per year---equivalent
to roughly 25\% of Spain's entire annual family and child policy
budget---for a programme whose cost per additional birth exceeds the
per-birth net fiscal contribution under all empirically plausible
parameter values. This opportunity cost should be a key factor in any
policy cost-benefit assessment.

\subsection{Distributional analysis and programme design}\label{sec:distributive}

The distributive implications of public funding depend on the economic
profile of existing private users and the design of the public
programme. Figure~\ref{fig:quintiles} shows estimated OC utilisation
rates by income quintile in the current private market and under two
alternative public funding designs, calibrated on the access gradients
documented by \citet{alonpinilla2021} and \citet{seiz2023}. The
statistics expose a sharply pro-rich profile in the current private
market: the estimated utilisation rate in the top income quintile is
approximately fourteen times that of the bottom quintile. A universal
public subsidy reduces this gradient but does not eliminate it, given
that informational barriers, geographic access, and cultural attitudes
towards ART are consistently associated with socioeconomic status. A
means-tested subsidy restricted to income quintiles Q1--Q3 achieves a
near-neutral distribution of programme gains while substantially
reducing the fiscal burden.

Figure~\ref{fig:concentration} shows the corresponding concentration
curves \citep{kakwani1977}. The private market yields a concentration
index of approximately 0.42, strongly pro-rich, calibrated on Spanish
income quintile shares (World Bank WDI 2023) and the Q5/Q1 access
gradient of 14 \citep{alonpinilla2021}. Universal public funding
reduces this to approximately 0.18; means-tested funding approaches
distributional neutrality (concentration index $\approx 0.05$). If the
declared goal of public OC funding is to improve equity of
reproductive access, a means-tested instrument is clearly preferable
on both efficiency and equity grounds.

Three alternative institutional designs should be assessed alongside
income targeting. First, direct public delivery through existing ART
units---rather than a demand-side subsidy to private clinics---would
integrate the programme with the governance and quality control regime
of the Spanish National Health System (SNS); however, with current
average waiting times of 339 days, expanding eligibility to elective
OC without a matching capacity expansion would likely delay access for
clinically indicated IVF. Second, a progressive co-payment (analogous
to Spain's pharmaceutical co-payment) could support partial cost
recovery from higher-income users while preserving access for
lower-income users. Third, any design should include mandatory
pre-treatment counselling by an independent clinician on age-specific
success probabilities and the realistic likelihood of utilisation, as
prospective users consistently overestimate both parameters in the
literature \citep{kynigopoulou2024,gambadauro2023}.

\subsection{Adverse events, provider incentives, and the
ICER}\label{sec:adverse}

Costs associated with adverse events should be incorporated in any
comprehensive budget estimate. Ovarian hyperstimulation syndrome (OHSS)
is the major risk of controlled ovarian stimulation: moderate OHSS
occurs in 3--6\% of stimulation cycles and severe OHSS requiring
hospitalisation in 0.5--2\% \citep{luke2012,pai2021}. In the base
scenario of 180,000 cycles per year (10\% uptake), severe OHSS would
generate approximately 900--3,600 hospitalisations annually, at an
average cost of \euro{}3,500--8,000 per episode based on
\citet{minsan2024} tariffs, implying an annual adverse event cost of
\euro{}3M--29M above direct procedure costs. Follicular puncture
complications (${\sim}0.3\%$ of retrievals) are less frequent but not
negligible at programme scale. Present evidence does not indicate higher
rates of congenital anomalies or adverse developmental outcomes in
vitrification-conceived children \citep{cobo2022,noyes2009}; however,
existing data are not sufficiently powered to detect rare outcomes at
the frequency that would become epidemiologically visible under a
large-scale public programme, whose long-term safety data would not be
available for two decades.

The Spanish ART market is predominantly private: approximately 78\% of
all ART cycles were undertaken in private institutions in 2021
\citep{sef2024registro}. A demand-side subsidy would expand the pool
of solvent demand, directly increasing private-clinic revenues.
\citet{deonandan2012} document that ART providers systematically
exaggerate the odds of successful treatment in counselling interactions,
and that the marketing of elective OC tends to conflate the probability
of frozen oocytes with the probability of a live birth---a distinction
that is highly consequential in practice. A public subsidy channelled
through private firms without price regulation, compulsory outcome
disclosure, or independent counselling obligations would expose the
public payer to supplier-induced demand \citep{evans1974} and to moral
hazard from women who freeze but would never use their oocytes, creating
pure deadweight cost. The regulatory conditions to minimise these
risks---tariff regulation, accreditation requirements, compulsory
pre-treatment counselling, public outcome reporting---are absent from
the current Spanish regulatory environment and from the PP policy
proposal \citep{partidopopular2025}.

The analysis is summarised by a single ICER, computed relative to the
comparator of no public subsidy. The base-case ICER is \euro{}433,333
per additional live birth, with a sensitivity range of \euro{}137,500
(optimistic) to above \euro{}1,000,000 (pessimistic). The Spanish NHS
framework operates on a quality-adjusted life year (QALY) basis with an
implicit threshold of approximately \euro{}25,000--30,000 per QALY
\citep{vallejotorres2018}. Under the assumption of 20 full-quality life
years per expected birth, the base-case cost per birth is equivalent to
\euro{}21,667 per QALY, which lies within the conventional threshold.
However, this calculation rests on highly uncertain long-run
macroeconomic projections; and even where the QALY cost falls within
the threshold range, the programme cannot be deemed fiscally
self-sustaining at any empirically meaningful parameter value: the
break-even utilisation rate under optimistic clinical assumptions is
approximately 3.8 times the empirical average. The QALY framing and
the fiscal framing therefore point in different directions, and the
latter is the more policy-relevant criterion for a programme explicitly
motivated on pro-natalist fiscal grounds.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------

This paper has conducted a decision-analytic economic evaluation of
publicly funded elective OC as proposed in the Spanish national policy
debate. The findings yield a consistent picture across all three
dimensions examined.

On cost-effectiveness, the analysis indicates that publicly funded
elective OC is unlikely to become a self-sustaining financial process
under empirically plausible utilisation and success rates. The gross
fiscal return per euro spent (\euro{}0.21 in the base case)
is well below break-even and dramatically below the \euro{}15.98
return estimated for clinical IVF by \citet{matorras2016}. The critical
difference is that clinical IVF is provided to infertile couples for
whom a live birth is genuinely additional; elective OC is provided
to women who may well have conceived by other means, and most of whom
will never use their stored oocytes. The low empirical utilisation
rate (5--12\%) is therefore not a contingent implementation failure
that improved programme design could remedy: it is a revealed
preference signal that the option has value to users primarily as
insurance, not as a pathway to a birth that would not otherwise
have occurred.

On budget impact, the annual cost of a national programme would run to
\euro{}470M at minimum (5\% uptake) and would exceed \euro{}1.87Bn
at 20\% uptake---a scale comparable to Spain's entire annual family
and child policy budget, for a programme that fails the fiscal
self-sustainability condition at all empirically meaningful parameter
values.

On distribution, a universal subsidy is moderately pro-rich; a
means-tested design targeting income quintiles Q1--Q3 achieves near
distributional neutrality at a substantially lower fiscal cost.

Three policy implications follow. First, publicly funded elective OC
should not be justified on pro-natalist or fiscal sustainability
grounds; such justification would require implausibly optimistic
assumptions about both programme uptake and demographic additionality.
If the policy moves forward, it should be articulated on autonomy and
equity principles---reducing financial barriers to a reproductive
option that lower-income women currently cannot access---with full
transparency about the low probability of future use and the moderate
increase in live birth probability the technology provides.
Second, any programme design must include compulsory pre-treatment
counselling by an independent clinician, public reporting of age- and
oocyte-stratified outcome data, and tariff regulation to discourage
supplier-induced demand in the largely private ART market; the absence
of these safeguards is a structural flaw, not a minor implementation
detail. Third, the \euro{}470M--1.87Bn annual cost of an OC subsidy
programme should be explicitly compared against evidence-based
alternatives such as affordable childcare and wage-replacement parental
leave, which have better-documented demographic effectiveness and
distributionally progressive effects \citep{gauthier2007,rindfuss2010}.

The salient issue of publicly funded OC is therefore redistribution
and access in the interest of policy, not a pro-natalist fiscal
instrument to be deployed.

\section{Decision-Tree Representation of the Cost
Model}\label{app:decisiontree}

This appendix presents a stylised decision tree of the economic logic
underpinning the cost model in Section~\ref{sec:model}. The tree
combines the behavioural parameters of Section~\ref{sec:parameters}
(uptake and utilisation rates) with the clinical outcome of
Section~\ref{sec:cost} (success rate) to trace the path from programme
enrolment to an additional live birth, clarifying how programme costs
are incurred at the vitrification stage regardless of whether oocytes
are ever used. The fiscal break-even condition
(Equation~\eqref{eq:fiscal}) is jointly determined by the behavioural
and clinical margins at each node.

Figure~\ref{fig:decision_tree} should be read as a schematic
representation of the analytic framework in
Section~\ref{sec:results}, not as a standalone empirical result.
